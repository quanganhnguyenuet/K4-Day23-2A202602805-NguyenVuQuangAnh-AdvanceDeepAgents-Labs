# World Models: Foundations, Competing Methods, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- Early world-model work framed the problem as learning a compact latent model of environment dynamics that can support control, including acting inside imagined rollouts rather than only from real interaction traces [1][2][3].
- A later branch of the literature emphasized structured latent state and systematic generalization, arguing that object-centric or compositional representations matter for world modeling beyond pixel prediction alone [4][2].
- Recent work has diversified into adaptable latent-action models, planning-integrated systems, memory-augmented diffusion models, and video-based simulators, suggesting that “world model” now spans both control-centric dynamics and generative scene prediction [5][6][7][8].
- Evaluation has become a central problem: newer benchmarks and benchmarks-in-progress focus on memory consistency, action control, long-horizon fidelity, and out-of-sight dynamics rather than only short-horizon visual realism [9][7][10].
- The literature still lacks a single agreed definition of what counts as a world model, and several sources explicitly separate predictive internal state models from broader video generation or language-agent abstractions [2][4][11][10].

## Background
World models are usually presented as learned internal representations of an environment that support prediction, planning, or action. In the classic formulation popularized in the 2018 “World Models” project, a controller can learn in a latent dream world produced by a learned environment model, making the model useful not only for forecasting but also for policy learning [1]. That framing builds on earlier self-supervised and recurrent model ideas that the 2020 retrospective links to the 1990–1991 period, when recurrent predictive systems were already being explored as differentiable world models and as components for dynamic reinforcement learning [3]. A related thread in model-based RL argued that a useful world model should capture objects and relations rather than only low-level pixels, because systematic generalization depends on structure in the latent state space [4].

The recent literature still uses the term in multiple ways. One line treats world models as compact latent dynamics for embodied control, where the key question is whether the latent state supports action-conditioned rollout and planning [2][5][6]. Another line, especially in video and 3D generation, treats world models as systems that synthesize coherent visual futures or explorable scenes; these systems may be evaluated on realism, temporal consistency, or interaction fidelity rather than on explicit control [7][8][12][10]. The sources here therefore support a plural view: a world model can be a control-oriented latent dynamics model, a memory-augmented predictor, or a generative simulator, but the evidence across families consistently returns to one theme, namely that prediction must remain useful under interventions, not merely in open-loop reconstruction [2][9][7][11].

## Foundations: latent dynamics, imagination, and structure
The 2018 “World Models” project remains the clearest canonical example of the latent-control view. Its central claim is that an agent can first learn an unsupervised compressed spatial-temporal representation of the environment, then train a compact policy inside the model’s “dream” environment [1]. This is important because it makes the world model an enabling component for control, not an end in itself. The same source family, together with the 2023 partial-observations paper, shows that later work continued to assume an action-conditioned predictive state that supports closed-loop interaction even when observations are incomplete [2].

The 2019 perspective on objects and systematic generalization argues that world models should not be judged only by aggregate prediction loss, because object structure is what lets agents transfer and recombine knowledge [4]. That paper connects world modeling to compositionality and to the possibility of learning abstract, reusable knowledge about the environment. The 2020 retrospective on 1990–1991 reinforces the historical point that these ideas are older than the 2018 project: recurrent self-supervised prediction and differentiable dynamics were already being explored as building blocks for reinforcement learning decades earlier [3]. Taken together, these sources support a foundational thesis: a world model is most compelling when it represents latent state in a way that can be queried by action and used for planning, not just for reconstruction [1][3][4].

A limitation of the foundational literature is that it does not settle the boundary between world models and other latent predictors. The 2023 work on partial observations emphasizes predictive state from real-world data, but its abstracted framing still leaves open how much object structure, memory, or explicit action modeling is required [2]. That ambiguity persists in later debates and survey-style overviews [11][10].

## Competing methods and recent progress
Recent work shows several competing directions rather than one dominant architecture. One path extends the latent-dynamics tradition toward adaptable control. AdaWorld introduces latent actions for adaptation, which suggests that compact action representations may reduce interaction requirements in new environments [5]. In the same control-oriented vein, Dual Preference Optimization for embodied task planning argues that world modeling can improve planning itself, using state prediction and action selection jointly with preference learning and tree search [6]. These works keep the basic assumption that a world model should be useful for policy improvement, but they differ in whether adaptation is driven by latent actions or by planning-time optimization [5][6].

A second path is memory-centric. The benchmark MIND evaluates memory consistency and action control, and Composition of Memory Experts for Diffusion World Models explicitly treats long-horizon consistency as a memory problem rather than only a dynamics problem [9][8]. This is a meaningful shift: instead of asking whether a model can predict the next frame, these works ask whether the model can preserve contextual state over longer horizons and across interruptions [9][8]. The evidence here suggests that memory has become a first-class design constraint for practical world models, especially when scenes evolve outside the current observation window [7][8].

A third path is video-world-modeling and generative simulation. LiveWorld targets out-of-sight dynamics in video world models by introducing a persistent global state, and FlowVid focuses on temporal consistency in video-to-video synthesis by combining spatial conditions with optical-flow cues [7][12]. These papers indicate that generative video systems are increasingly treated as world models when they preserve state over time, though their criteria remain different from embodied-control benchmarks. The key limitation is that visual continuity does not automatically imply faithful world-state tracking: the open problem is whether the model remembers what the world is doing when it is not directly visible [7][12].

Finally, recent overviews explicitly map the space of approaches into multiple competing families. The Themesis overview argues that “world models” now include distinct approaches rather than one unified recipe, while WorldLens proposes a unified benchmark spanning generation, reconstruction, action-following, downstream tasks, and human preference [11][10]. Even if these are not primary research papers in the same sense as the others, they reflect the current landscape: the field is fragmenting into specialized systems with different assumptions, targets, and evaluation criteria [11][10].

## Evaluation, benchmarks, and what they actually measure
Evaluation is where the field’s ambiguity becomes concrete. The MIND benchmark is designed around memory consistency and action control, implying that a useful world model must be stable over time and responsive to interventions [9]. LiveWorld shifts attention to out-of-sight dynamics, which is a different criterion: the model must maintain state continuity even when the relevant object or agent leaves the field of view [7]. WorldLens broadens the evaluation space further by including generation, reconstruction, action-following, downstream tasks, and human preference across many dimensions [10].

These evaluation choices reflect an important methodological split. Some metrics primarily measure perceptual plausibility or frame-level coherence, while others attempt to measure whether the model encodes actionable world state. The recent sources suggest that the latter is harder but more meaningful for “world model” claims [9][7][10]. The 2023 partial-observations paper also points toward the same issue: if the input is incomplete, then good performance requires inference about hidden state, not just copying visible patterns [2]. The 2019 object-structure perspective provides a conceptual reason to care: models that fail to represent objects and relations may score well on local prediction yet fail at systematic generalization [4].

A broader concern is that benchmark scores can conflate many properties at once. Video-synthesis systems may appear strong because they produce sharp frames, but that does not prove they track causal state, preserve identity, or support control [12][10]. Conversely, control-oriented benchmarks may reward policy recovery without revealing whether the latent model truly “understands” the environment [6][9]. The most defensible conclusion from the retrieved sources is that the field still needs evaluation suites that jointly test memory, action faithfulness, hidden-state inference, and downstream control, rather than optimizing any single score in isolation [9][7][10].

## Trends and open problems
The sources point to three clear trends. First, world modeling is moving from short-horizon prediction toward long-horizon persistence: memory, out-of-sight dynamics, and state continuity are now central concerns [9][7][8]. Second, the field is converging on hybrid systems that mix representation learning, planning, and control, rather than treating world modeling as a standalone prediction module [5][6][11]. Third, evaluation is broadening from perceptual quality to action-following, utility, and preference-based judgments [9][10].

The open problems are equally clear. There is still no stable, universally accepted definition of what exactly counts as a world model, and the retrieved sources repeatedly indicate that latent dynamics, video generation, and agent memory are adjacent but not identical tasks [2][11][10]. Long-horizon reliability remains weak, especially when the model must track hidden objects, recover after interruptions, or maintain consistency across many steps [7][8]. Another gap is causal adequacy: a model may generate plausible futures without representing the interventions that produce them [2][4][12]. Finally, the field lacks evaluation protocols that directly test whether a learned representation supports planning and systematic generalization in the way the foundational literature hoped it would [4][6][9]. In synthesis, the research frontier is no longer about whether world models can predict one next step; it is about whether they can hold together memory, action, structure, and usable control over long horizons [5][9][7][10].
