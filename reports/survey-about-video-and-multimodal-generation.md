# Survey of video and multimodal generation

## TL;DR
- Diffusion remains the clearest technical foundation in the retrieved evidence: DDPM frames generation as a latent-variable process trained with a weighted variational objective tied to denoising score matching, and later efficiency work continues to organize the field around the same sampling/training tradeoffs. [1][2][3]
- Early multimodal generation work in the catalog centers on unified video-language pretraining, while later sources emphasize larger video-text corpora and reusable video-text representations as the scaling substrate for both understanding and generation. [4][5]
- Recent progress is less about a single generator and more about systems: unified generation-understanding models, reusable distillation, prompt-enhancer evaluation, and specialized benchmarks for hard subdomains such as data video, camera motion, and animation. [6][7][8][9][10][11][12][13][14]
- The strongest open problems in the retrieved sources are evaluation confounding, temporal and physical reasoning, controllability, and efficiency; safety and misuse are present in the catalog but remain much less evidenced than quality/efficiency concerns. [8][9][10][15]

## Background
Video and multimodal generation combine two hard problems: generating temporally coherent visual sequences and aligning those sequences to language or other conditioning signals. The retrieved evidence supports diffusion as the dominant representational foundation. DDPM describes diffusion probabilistic models as latent-variable models and explicitly links training to denoising score matching and Langevin dynamics; it also frames generation as progressive lossy decompression, which helps explain why diffusion-style refinement became a durable default for high-dimensional media generation. [1][2]

The multimodal side of the field begins in the retrieved sources with unified video-language pretraining. UniVL is described as a unified video and language pre-training model for multimodal understanding and generation, which is important because it treats generation not as a separate decoder-only add-on but as part of a shared representation learning problem. [4] Later catalog entries strengthen the same assumption with scale: InternVid is presented as a large-scale video-text multimodal dataset paired with ViCLIP, a video-text representation model, to enable advanced understanding and generation tasks. In other words, the field’s multimodal foundation in these sources is not a specific architecture alone but a combination of shared latent spaces, large paired corpora, and transfer across understanding and generation. [5]

A second background theme is that the field is trying to unify previously separate pipelines. Recent sources describe generation-centric unification, where a video generator becomes the base for understanding as well, and where single transformers or bidirectional training schemes are used to reduce fragmentation across tasks. [6][11][14] That unification effort matters because the same model is increasingly expected to perform generation, editing, reasoning, and in some cases downstream control. The evidence base here is strongest for diffusion-centric and transformer-centric unification, but weaker for classic tokenization milestones and early autoregressive video systems, which were not well covered in the seeded catalog. That gap should be treated as an evidence limitation, not as evidence that those methods are unimportant. [1][4][5]

## Diffusion as the core generative substrate
The most stable technical backbone in the retrieved sources is diffusion. DDPM gives the field its canonical framing: generation via a latent-variable diffusion process, optimized with a weighted variational objective connected to denoising score matching and Langevin dynamics. [1][2] The practical significance of this framing is that it turns synthesis into iterative denoising rather than one-shot prediction, which is particularly attractive when the output space is high-dimensional, such as images and videos. The retrieved evidence does not provide a video-specific diffusion primer, but later sources clearly assume diffusion remains the default video generation substrate. [3][6][7]

The 2024 efficiency survey reinforces this picture by organizing diffusion work around architecture design, training, inference, and deployment rather than around a wholly different generative paradigm. That is itself an important result: diffusion is no longer presented merely as a modeling choice, but as an engineering stack whose bottlenecks are speed, cost, and deployment. [3] The same concern appears more concretely in LongLive-Plug, which says video diffusion models are increasingly specialized and often require a distillation stage for each target model. Its answer is reusable LoRA-based capabilities for single-pass classifier-free guidance, few-step sampling, and long-context error correction. [7]

This suggests a current tradeoff: diffusion still supplies quality and controllability, but deployment increasingly depends on reducing sampling cost and avoiding per-model retraining. The catalog’s evidence is therefore more about making diffusion practical than replacing it. [3][7] Uni-ViGU supports the same reading from another angle: it extends a diffusion-based video generator with unified flow matching and bidirectional training to support both generation and understanding. Even when newer training objectives appear, they are still grafted onto a diffusion foundation rather than discarding it outright. [6]

A notable limitation in the retrieved sources is that they do not include strong primary evidence for non-diffusion video generation families at the same level of detail. That means any broader claim that “the field has moved beyond diffusion” would be unsupported here. The available evidence instead points to diffusion as the dominant base model family, with newer work focusing on efficiency, reuse, and task unification. [3][6][7]

## Multimodal pretraining and shared video-text representations
The multimodal generation story in the retrieved catalog begins with shared video-language pretraining. UniVL is explicitly framed as a unified video and language pre-training model for multimodal understanding and generation. That matters because it establishes a design principle that persists in later sources: video generation is not only about predicting frames, but about learning a representation that can serve both understanding and generation tasks. [4]

InternVid extends this logic by shifting the bottleneck from architecture to data scale. It is described as a large-scale video-text multimodal dataset, paired with ViCLIP, a video-text representation model based on ViT-L, enabling advanced understanding and generation tasks. [5] The implication is that multimodal generation improves not just by clever decoding, but by learning from large, aligned corpora that connect temporal visual content with language. This is especially important for video, where the alignment problem is richer than in static image generation: captions, dialogue, actions, and scene evolution all need to be grounded in time. [5]

Recent unified models continue this direction. HaploOmni is described as a unified single transformer for multimodal video understanding and generation, and Uni-ViGU describes generation-centric unification with bidirectional training. [11][6] These sources show two competing but related views of multimodal progress. One view is transformer unification: a single model handles multiple modalities and tasks. The other is generation-first unification: a strong generator becomes the anchor for understanding. The catalog supports both, but does not provide enough evidence to claim one has decisively won. [6][11]

The unresolved issue is representation granularity. The sources repeatedly imply that successful models need compressed or shared latent spaces, but they differ in how those spaces are constructed: diffusion denoising trajectories, transformer hidden states, or specialized video-text encoders. [1][4][5][6][11] Because the catalog lacks primary papers on classic video tokenization milestones, the evidence here cannot fully trace the path from discrete video tokens to today’s unified systems. That is a real gap in the retrieved record, and it limits how confidently one can narrate the field’s architectural genealogy.

## Recent progress: specialization, controllability, and reusable components
Recent sources suggest that the field is fragmenting into specialized capability modules built on top of strong generative backbones. LongLive-Plug is a clear example: it aims to make a base model reusable across downstream video-generation tasks by learning a set of transferable LoRA capabilities. [7] This is a major practical shift from training a bespoke model for every new setting. It also implies that once a video generator is good enough, the next bottleneck becomes adaptation cost and sampling latency rather than only raw fidelity. [7]

Another thread is control. TKCAM generates camera trajectories from text and keyframes using generative masked modeling and hierarchical motion tokens. That source is important because it shows how generation is being decomposed into controllable subproblems: instead of generating an entire video monolithically, the system first models camera motion with structured latent tokens. [16] This echoes broader trends in video synthesis toward separating scene content, motion, and cinematography. The retrieved catalog does not include direct evidence that such factorization universally improves generation, but it does show that controllable motion generation is now a distinct research target. [16]

The catalog also includes Playable Game Generation, which combines autoregressive DiT-based diffusion models with a playability evaluation framework to generate interactive games. Although this is adjacent to video generation rather than a pure video method, it illustrates a broader trend: generative models are being judged by downstream temporal behavior, not just visual realism. [12] In the same spirit, Self-correction Optimization for Interleaved Multimodal Generation emphasizes preserving visual subjects, temporal consistency, and physical plausibility in interleaved outputs. [14] Together, these sources show that recent progress is increasingly about composition, control, and continuity across steps or modalities. [12][16][14]

A final recent trend is domain specialization. MagicAnime targets cartoon animation generation with a hierarchically annotated multitasking dataset and benchmarks, which suggests that general video generators are still not sufficient for all stylistic or structural domains. [13] This specialization pattern fits the rest of the evidence: the field is not converging on one universal benchmark or one universal generator, but on a family of task-adapted systems with reusable cores. [7][11][13]

## Evaluation and open problems
Evaluation is one of the clearest open problems in the retrieved sources. PEBench argues that existing prompt-enhancer evaluation depends on rendered videos, which is expensive and confounded by downstream generator behavior. Its proposal is a direct benchmark for text-to-video, image-to-video, and reference-to-video prompt enhancement. This is a strong sign that the field is moving evaluation upstream: instead of scoring only final videos, researchers want to isolate the quality of the conditioning or planning module itself. [8]

The same principle appears in DataVista, which says existing benchmarks target either general videos or static charts and therefore miss data-video understanding, especially the need to read animated charts and integrate evidence across time. [9] WOVEN makes a similar complaint from a reasoning perspective: current benchmarks document spatial, embodied, physical, and temporal deficits separately, but do not support controlled comparisons across scenes, actions, and reasoning operations. [10] These papers collectively suggest that video/multimodal evaluation is becoming more task-specific because generic metrics are too blunt to diagnose failure modes. [8][9][10]

The open problems named or implied across the catalog are consistent. First, temporal reasoning remains difficult, whether the target is coherent video generation, visual transition reasoning, or interleaved multimodal generation. [10][14] Second, physical plausibility and controllability remain fragile, which is why sources on camera motion, prompt enhancement, and self-correction all focus on structure rather than only appearance. [16][14][8] Third, efficiency is still a major constraint: the efficiency survey and LongLive-Plug both show that faster sampling and deployment-friendly adaptation are central design goals, not afterthoughts. [3][7]

Safety and robustness are less well covered in the retrieved record. Dialect robustness appears in one 2025 benchmark, but the catalog offers little direct evidence on copyright leakage, harmful content generation, or policy alignment for video systems. [15] Because of that, any broad statement that “video generation safety is solved” or even “well studied” would be unsupported here. The honest conclusion is narrower: the retrieved sources strongly support ongoing work on efficiency, controllability, and benchmark design, while safety remains under-evidenced in this particular corpus. [3][7][8][10][15]

## Trends and open problems
The retrieved literature points to four connected trends. First, diffusion remains the main generative substrate, but practical work now centers on making it faster, more reusable, and more compatible with downstream specialization. [1][3][7] Second, multimodal generation is increasingly unified with understanding, whether through shared pretraining, generation-first backbones, or single-transformer systems. [4][5][6][11] Third, the field is shifting toward structured control: prompt enhancers, camera motion, and self-correction mechanisms all break generation into more manageable units. [8][16][14] Fourth, evaluation is fragmenting into task-specific benchmarks because rendered-video scoring alone cannot separate generator quality from conditioning or reasoning quality. [8][9][10]

The main open problems are correspondingly concrete. One is temporal consistency: models still struggle with maintaining coherent motion, causality, and long-range state across frames or interleaved outputs. [10][14] Another is physical plausibility, especially when models are asked to combine narration, camera motion, and fine-grained visual content. [16][14] A third is controllability: the field wants models that obey text, keyframes, prompts, or structured motion constraints without losing diversity or fidelity. [8][16] A fourth is efficient deployment, because high-quality generation remains expensive to sample and adapt. [3][7]

A final gap is evidentiary. The seeded catalog gives strong coverage of diffusion-based generation, unified video-language pretraining, recent control modules, and benchmark proposals, but it does not adequately cover classic video-tokenization milestones, early autoregressive video generation, or safety-focused evaluation. [1][4][5] That means the survey can make a confident statement about current diffusion- and transformer-centered practice, but not a complete historical claim about every major family in the field. The honest synthesis is that video and multimodal generation is currently being built around reusable generative cores, shared video-text representations, and more discriminating evaluation, while the hardest unresolved issues remain temporal reasoning, physical plausibility, controllability, and affordable deployment. [3][6][7][8][10][11][14]
