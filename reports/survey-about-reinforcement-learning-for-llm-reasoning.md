# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Foundations, Recent Methods, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- The core RL-for-LLM template comes from RLHF: a pretrained language model is steered by a learned reward model or human preference signal, with PPO and a KL penalty to keep the policy near the reference model. This template is explicit in early work on human preferences and instruction following. [1][2][3]
- Recent reasoning-focused RL shifts the reward target from general helpfulness to verifiable or rule-based signals, as in DeepSeek-R1 and related 2025 methods that emphasize correctness, formatting constraints, and more efficient group-relative updates. [4][5][6][7][8]
- Several 2025 papers suggest that gains in reasoning RL depend strongly on training efficiency choices, sampling criteria, and data/domain match, rather than on a single universal recipe. [5][6][7][8]
- Evaluation is now a central bottleneck: contamination, ceiling effects, and pass@1-only reporting can exaggerate progress, so recent critiques recommend uncontaminated benchmarks, stress tests, pass@k, and diagnostic metrics such as OPG. [9][10]

## Background
Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning inherits its basic structure from RLHF. In the early human-preference line, a pretrained language model is treated as the policy, a reward model is learned from preference data, and policy optimization is regularized so the model does not drift too far from the starting point. The foundational 2019/2020 work on learning from human preferences presents this setup as a way to apply RL to language tasks by learning reward from judgments rather than from an environment reward stream. [1] The later instruction-following work makes the same design more operational: it uses supervised fine-tuning, reward-model training, and then PPO with an explicit KL term that keeps the policy near the reference model. [2] PPO itself matters because it supplies the clipped surrogate objective that made this kind of policy optimization practical and stable for large neural policies. [3]

This background is important for reasoning because the later reasoning-RL methods do not discard the template; they narrow the reward definition. WebGPT is an early example of RL being used for a structured task where factuality and citation behavior matter, showing that RL can shape longer-horizon language behavior beyond simple next-token imitation. [11] OpenAI’s instruction-following summary reinforces the same general point: RLHF is used to align outputs to user intent, not merely to improve likelihood under pretraining. [12] In the reasoning setting, the key change is that the reward becomes more directly tied to verifiable solution correctness or tightly constrained answer formats, which makes RL useful for math, code, and other tasks where objective signals are available. [4][8]

## Foundational RLHF and optimization assumptions
The foundational assumption behind RL-for-LLM reasoning is that language generation can be cast as sequential decision-making with a policy over tokens and trajectories, where reward is available through preference modeling or verification. The early preference-learning paper argues that human judgment can supply a practical reward source for language tasks, replacing the need for a fully specified environment reward. [1] In instruction-following RLHF, the model is first adapted with supervised demonstrations and then optimized against a learned reward model, with a KL penalty to preserve fluency and prevent reward hacking or catastrophic drift. [2] That combination is not incidental: it encodes the assumption that a base LM already contains useful linguistic competence, and RL should nudge it rather than relearn language from scratch. [2][3]

PPO is the canonical optimization method in this lineage because it constrains policy updates through a clipped surrogate objective, reducing the instability that would otherwise arise when optimizing large autoregressive models against learned rewards. [3] This stability concern is central to reasoning RL as well, because reasoning trajectories are long and reward signals are often sparse or delayed. The practical consequence is that modern reasoning-RL papers still retain the same design vocabulary: reference policy, KL control, reward model or verifiable reward, and a policy update algorithm designed to be conservative. [2][3][4]

The resulting framework is best thought of as a tradeoff. Stronger optimization pressure can improve performance on targeted reasoning tasks, but too much pressure risks mode collapse, verbosity artifacts, or overfitting to reward proxies. That tradeoff is explicit in the preference-based and instruction-following work, and it remains visible in recent reasoning systems that use rule-based rewards and reference-policy regularization. [1][2][4]

## From generic RLHF to reasoning-specific reward design
The most visible shift in 2025 reasoning RL is the move from generic helpfulness rewards to task-verifiable rewards. DeepSeek-R1 reports large-scale RL without supervised fine-tuning for its first-stage reasoning model and describes a reward design based on correctness and format, with a reference-policy KL term and a group-relative optimization procedure. [4] The archived record corroborates the same broad setup and dates the report to January 2025. [13] The important conceptual change is not simply that RL is used, but that reward is now tightly coupled to problems where the system can check an answer programmatically or with narrow rules, making reasoning more directly trainable. [4][13]

That same direction appears in later 2025 work. The memory-constraint paper describes S-GRPO and T-SPMO as techniques that improve reasoning performance and stability under compute and memory limits, suggesting that the optimization recipe matters as much as the reward source. [5] The selective-rollout paper argues that online filtering can skip uninformative prompts and improve training efficiency without hurting final performance, again indicating that the data-selection layer is part of the method, not just an implementation detail. [7] The competence-difficulty alignment paper adds another layer: it claims sample efficiency improves when prompt difficulty is matched to model competence, implying that the RL curriculum should be aware of current skill level rather than sample prompts uniformly. [6]

A reasonable synthesis is that reasoning-RL is increasingly a systems problem. Reward design, rollout selection, difficulty scheduling, and memory efficiency all shape the final model. The evidence here supports the claim that recent gains are not attributable to one single algorithmic advance; they are the result of a stack of choices that make sparse, verifiable rewards usable at scale. [4][5][6][7]

## Competing methods and recent progress
Not all recent progress is pure RL. The cross-domain paper argues that reasoning improvements differ across tasks and domains, so a single training recipe does not transfer uniformly. [8] That matters because it weakens the idea that one reward-shaping trick can solve reasoning broadly. Instead, the paper’s framing suggests that the training mix should reflect the domain structure of the target task. [8]

Within the RL family, GRPO-style updates have become important because they reduce reliance on a value network and fit well with group-based reward signals. DeepSeek-R1 is the clearest primary source here, but the later federated GRPO paper indicates that GRPO is now an explicit method family being extended to distributed settings, which is evidence that the update rule has become a reusable primitive. [4][14] The federated paper is also useful as a signpost: if a method is being adapted to privacy-constrained environments, it usually means the community sees it as a stable core rather than a one-off trick. [14]

The broader competing-method landscape includes approaches that may replace or complement policy-gradient RL in reasoning workflows, but the retrieved notes here only provide partial evidence for those alternatives. The notes mention that RL is being supplemented or replaced by preference optimization, process supervision, and test-time/self-improvement methods, but the retrieved source set does not include enough direct primary evidence to compare those methods rigorously in this report. That is a real retrieval gap, not a conclusion. The safely supported claim is narrower: recent RL papers focus on making on-policy reasoning training more efficient and more tightly coupled to verifiable outcomes, while cross-domain results warn that these improvements are domain-sensitive. [5][6][7][8]

## Evaluation and open problems
Evaluation remains the weakest part of the literature. A 2025 contamination-focused paper warns that gains on popular reasoning benchmarks can be misleading if the benchmarks overlap with pretraining data, and it argues that contamination can make RL results look like reasoning improvement when they are partly memorization. [9] This is especially relevant when claims are reported only on a narrow benchmark set, because a model that has seen similar data may appear to improve even if the underlying reasoning capability has not changed much. [9]

A 2026 ACL Findings paper adds a different critique: held-out benchmark scores may not separate methods well once models are strong enough, because training on the benchmark train split can approach training on the test split. It introduces the Oracle Performance Gap (OPG) to measure whether a benchmark actually distinguishes train and test optimization. [10] That is a meaningful shift: the question is no longer only “did the score rise?” but “does this benchmark still diagnose generalization under RL training?” [10]

Another recent critique finds that RLVR can improve pass@1 while still lagging at high-k sampling against the base model, implying that the method may improve answer selection efficiency more than it expands the underlying reasoning distribution. [8] This is a subtle but important evaluation result, because pass@1 can reward sharper selection while hiding failures of breadth, calibration, or exploration. [8] The workshop page on holistic evaluation for reasoning about action, change, and planning similarly argues that binary success alone is too coarse for sequential reasoning tasks, and that non-binary metrics may better capture closeness to ground truth. [14]

The open problems that follow are concrete. First, the field still needs benchmarks that are both uncontaminated and difficult enough to avoid ceiling effects. [9][10] Second, there is no consensus on whether RL primarily improves new reasoning capacity or mostly improves sampling efficiency and answer selection. [8] Third, training recipes remain domain-sensitive: competence-aware sampling, rollout filtering, and memory-aware optimization all help, but the evidence so far does not identify a universal best practice. [5][6][7][8] Finally, evaluation should track more than single-number accuracy, especially on tasks where a partially correct trajectory may be informative even if the final answer is not exact. [10][14]

## Trends and open problems
- RL for reasoning has moved from generic helpfulness optimization to verifiable or rule-based reward design, but the support for that shift is strongest in a small number of primary sources. [4][13][8]
- Efficiency is now a first-class concern: recent methods explicitly study memory, rollout selection, and curriculum/difficulty matching, suggesting that training dynamics matter as much as reward design. [5][6][7]
- The strongest current evaluation warnings concern contamination, weak benchmark separation, and pass@1 overinterpretation; these problems can make RL progress look larger than it really is. [9][10][8]
- Open problems remain whether RL adds genuinely new reasoning capability, how to benchmark cross-domain transfer, and how to compare RL against non-RL alternatives fairly; the retrieved evidence supports the existence of these issues, but not a settled resolution. [8][9][10][14]
